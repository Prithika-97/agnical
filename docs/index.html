Punjab and Haryana's farm fires moved later in the day. The satellites that count them didn't.

NASA's fire satellites pass over Punjab and Haryana at fixed hours. AgniCal joins their 23-year record into one scale, and shows when the count stopped measuring how much is burned.

What the count says

Before 2012 only MODIS existed. Its pixels are about 1 km wide, so it sees fewer fires than VIIRS (375 m). Joining the two raw records makes 2012 look like a jump in burning. AgniCal learns how MODIS observations translate into VIIRS-equivalent counts during the years both flew, and rebuilds 2003–2011 on the VIIRS scale.

When is the peak? As far as polar orbit can see

On a day when two satellites pass at different hours, the ratio of what they see depends on the shape of that day's burning, not on how much burned. AgniCal fits that ratio across every day of every season with all five satellites, and reports the peak hour only where satellite viewing times pin it down.

How AgniCal works

  1. Data. Every active-fire detection from NASA FIRMS for MODIS (Terra, Aqua; 2003–2026) and VIIRS (Suomi NPP, NOAA-20, NOAA-21), cut to the official borders of Punjab and Haryana and their districts. Low-confidence and non-vegetation detections are removed.
  2. One scale. Detections are gridded to 0.1° cells and 8-day periods. A gradient-boosted model with a Poisson loss learns how many Suomi NPP detections a cell has, given MODIS evidence: Terra and Aqua day and night counts, fire radiative power, neighbouring cells and adjacent periods. It is trained only on 2012–2022, before Aqua's orbit drifted, and checked by leaving out one whole season at a time.
  3. Time of day. Each satellite pass is a snapshot at a known hour. Conditioning on each day's total removes how much burned and leaves the shape of the day's burning, estimated with a day-matched Poisson model and a day-level bootstrap.
  4. Timing breaks. A season is flagged when at least two of the three model-free indicators leave the 2012–2022 range by more than three robust standard deviations. In flagged seasons, counts from early-afternoon satellites are shown but not compared with earlier years.

Limitations

  • Active-fire detections are snapshots, not burned area. Cloud, haze and fires that start and end between passes are missed by every polar satellite.
  • Polar satellites cannot measure burning after about 15:00. Where the peak lies beyond that, AgniCal reports only how late it is at least. A full correction needs all-day geostationary data (Meteosat-9 over the Indian Ocean, INSAT-3D/3DR, GEO-KOMPSAT-2A), which is the next layer to add.
  • Aqua's drift also changes its sun and viewing angles, which could affect what it detects. A shift in burning time is the most likely explanation because three independent indicators and two geostationary studies agree, but the Aqua ratio alone would not prove it.
  • District borders are from 2021, before Malerkotla and Charkhi Dadri were created; their fires are counted under the districts they were formed from.
  • Near-real-time 2026 files carry no fire-type field, so a few static heat sources may remain in this season's counts.
