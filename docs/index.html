We checked every state in India that has enough fires. Only in Punjab and Haryana did farm burning slip out of the satellites' sight.

NASA's fire satellites pass over India at the same hours every day, and those hours have slowly drifted. AgniCal puts 23 years of their records on one scale and runs the same check on every state: is the count still comparable with earlier years? In most places it is. In Punjab and Haryana, farmers now burn later in the day than the satellites look, so the count no longer tells the whole story. We also work out a minimum for what it now misses.

What is new here. Scientists at ISRO and NASA have already shown, with satellites that watch all day, that burning now peaks in the late afternoon. We wanted to know whether NASA's regular polar satellites could see the same thing on their own, and whether the satellites' own drifting orbits could explain it. They can't. On top of that, AgniCal adds a few things: a way to use Terra and Aqua's drift as a scanner of the fire day, results for the season happening right now, a blind-spot score for every district, and a checker anyone can run on their own region.

A day of fire, scanned by two drifting satellites

NASA stopped keeping Terra and Aqua on their old orbits, so their passing times have been sliding apart: Terra earlier each year, Aqua later. People usually see this as damage to the record. We use it as a tool instead. Both carry the same sensor, so each season they look at the same fires at two different hours, and the hours change every year. Next to Suomi NPP, which stays near 13:30, the points show how fire activity rises and falls through the day, with no all-day satellite needed.

The test needs at least eight seasons between 2012 and 2022 with 300 or more Suomi NPP daytime fires each. The same two indicators and thresholds are used as everywhere on this page.

The proof: is the shift real, or did the satellites change?

Farmers burning later is not our discovery. Scientists at ISRO and NASA showed it in 2025 and 2026 using satellites that watch all day. Our question is about the record everyone actually uses: can the regular polar satellites pick up the change on their own, is it really the burning that changed, and how much does the count miss now?

There's a fair reason to doubt it. Aqua's orbit drifted later and Terra's earlier in the same years, and a satellite looking at a different hour sees a different number of fires even if nothing on the ground changed. So we ran every test twice: once on Punjab and Haryana, and once on the farm fires just across the border in Pakistani Punjab, which the same satellites see on the same passes.

When is the peak? As far as polar orbit can see

On a day when two satellites pass at different hours, the ratio of what they see depends on the shape of that day's burning, not on how much burned. AgniCal fits that ratio across every day of every season with all five satellites, and reports the peak hour only where satellite viewing times pin it down.

A floor under the real count

Until 2022, Aqua and Suomi NPP passed at almost the same minute, so one Aqua fire meant a steady number of Suomi NPP fires. Aqua now passes about an hour and a half later. Using the old ratio to convert its count tells us what Suomi NPP would have counted at Aqua's hour. Burning peaks later still, so treat this as a minimum, not an estimate of the total.

Where a fixed MODIS-to-VIIRS conversion can be trusted

To harmonize two sensors, you turn one's count into what the other would have counted. We worked out that conversion for each state and season using 2012–2022, before the drift, then applied it to 2023–2025 and compared the answer with what Suomi NPP really counted. Where the error stays small, the conversion is safe to use. Where it grows, it has stopped working, and the region should be flagged instead of converted.

Checked against a published geostationary study

There is one outside check we could find: Jethva's 2026 study of the same region using Korea's GK-2A satellite, which looks every hour and so doesn't depend on when a satellite passes. It estimates that VIIRS missed roughly 56 percent of fires in 2024 and 64 percent in 2025, and that burning peaks around 16:00 to 18:00 IST. We never used GK-2A data, so this is independent of our numbers.

What the count says

Before 2012 only MODIS existed. Its pixels are about 1 km wide, so it sees fewer fires than VIIRS (375 m). Simply joining the two records makes 2012 look like a sudden jump in burning. AgniCal learns how MODIS translates into VIIRS-equivalent counts during the years both flew, and rebuilds 2003–2011 on the VIIRS scale.

Who this is for

Fire counts from these satellites are published daily in India and used to judge whether burning is falling. AgniCal's outputs are made for the people who publish and use those counts.

CREAMS laboratory, IARI

Publishes the daily paddy-residue burning bulletin from these satellites. The timing-break flag and the late-view floor can be printed beside each season's count, using data the laboratory already holds.

Commission for Air Quality Management, state pollution boards

Use year-on-year counts to judge districts. In flagged seasons a district's count is not comparable with its count before 2023; the calendar tool marks these seasons for every district.

Air-quality forecasters for Delhi

Emission inputs built from early-afternoon fire counts understate smoke in flagged seasons. The floor gives a minimum scaling, season by season.

Anyone who uses a MODIS fire record, anywhere

Aqua's drift is global. The checker above tells you, from your own FIRMS files, whether your region's count can still be compared with earlier years.

Researchers and journalists

The harmonized 2003–2025 record, with uncertainty and flags, is one file: agnical_record.csv. The method is not tied to one region: the same timing test ran unchanged on every state of India.

How AgniCal works

  1. Data. Every active-fire detection from NASA FIRMS for MODIS (Terra, Aqua; 2003–2026) and VIIRS (Suomi NPP, NOAA-20, NOAA-21), cut to the borders of Punjab and Haryana and their districts. Detections in the rest of the download box are kept as comparison regions. Low-confidence and non-vegetation detections are removed.
  2. One scale. Detections are gridded to 0.1° cells and 8-day periods. A gradient-boosted model with a Poisson loss learns how many Suomi NPP detections a cell has, given MODIS evidence: Terra and Aqua day and night counts, fire radiative power, neighbouring cells and adjacent periods. It is trained only on 2012–2022, before Aqua's orbit drifted, and checked by leaving out one whole season at a time.
  3. Time of day. Each satellite pass is a snapshot at a known hour. Conditioning on each day's total removes how much burned and leaves the shape of the day's burning, estimated with a day-matched Poisson model and a day-level bootstrap.
  4. Timing breaks. Two indicators need no model: Aqua fires per Suomi NPP fire, and the share of Suomi NPP fires seen on its night pass. A season is flagged when both leave the 2012–2022 range by more than three robust standard deviations, and confirmed when the area also moved further than a comparison region on both (95% interval above 1). There are two comparisons: the strip of Pakistani Punjab, and all Indian states with no flag. In flagged seasons, counts are shown but not compared with earlier years.
  5. Floor. Aqua's daytime count times the 2012–2022 Suomi NPP-to-Aqua ratio. The range uses the lowest and highest yearly ratio of those eleven seasons.

Limitations

  • Active-fire detections are snapshots, not burned area. Cloud, haze and fires that start and end between passes are missed by every polar satellite.
  • Polar satellites cannot measure burning after about 15:00. Where the peak lies beyond that, AgniCal reports only how late it is at least. A full correction needs all-day geostationary data (Meteosat-9 over the Indian Ocean, INSAT-3D/3DR, GEO-KOMPSAT-2A), which is the next layer to add.
  • The comparison region is a narrow strip of Pakistani Punjab inside the download box, with about a tenth of the study area's fires. It controls for the satellites, not for weather or crop calendar differences across the border.
  • The two indicators are not fully independent: both rest on Suomi NPP's daytime count. The night share uses Suomi NPP alone, so it is unaffected by Aqua's or Terra's drift.
  • The floor assumes the 2012–2022 Aqua-to-Suomi NPP ratio would still hold if both looked at the same hour. Across the border, where burning did not shift, the ratio stayed at its old level through Aqua's drift, which supports this.
  • The 2026 season is judged on 1 October to 2 November only, from near-real-time data, which NASA later replaces with a standard product. In the checker, an incomplete season is shown but compared with full seasons, so read it with care.
  • The district blind-spot index rests on small counts and has no interval.
  • The all-India run uses MODIS and Suomi NPP only, with Suomi NPP fires counted per 0.1° cell and assigned to the state of the cell centre, so a small share near state borders falls to the neighbour.
  • We couldn't get geostationary fire data during the hackathon, so the 16:00–18:00 peak is quoted from published studies, not computed here.
